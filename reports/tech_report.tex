\begin{abstract}
This report documents a from-scratch implementation and measurement study of the modern
diffusion stack. No \texttt{diffusers}, \texttt{flash\_attn} or pretrained generative
component is used: DDPM, DDIM, a diffusion transformer (DiT) with adaLN-Zero conditioning,
and rectified flow are all implemented in this repository, together with the training
pipeline, the sampler suite and the evaluation code. The study is organized as a controlled
2$\times$2 design (UNet vs.\ DiT $\times$ $\epsilon$-prediction vs.\ rectified flow) at two
scales, MNIST $28\times28$ and CelebA $64\times64$, with every cell trained under one frozen
protocol. Headline results: DDIM at 50 function evaluations matches the 1000-step ancestral
sampler on CelebA (FID $11.45$ vs.\ $11.37$) at $20\times$ the sampling throughput,
and rectified flow dominates the quality--NFE frontier on MNIST (FID $0.88$ at 64
evaluations, versus $4.32$ for the best $\epsilon$-prediction sampler at 50). The same
deterministic sampler that improves the UNet collapses on the transformer: its FID grows from
$49$ to $672$ as NFE goes $10\rightarrow50$, against $33$ for 1000-step ancestral sampling of
the same weights. An $\eta$ sweep shows the failure is the missing noise injection. On the
systems side, bf16 buys $3.15\times$ step time and $1.6\times$ memory over
fp32 while \texttt{torch.compile} buys nothing ($1.00\times$), and a kernel-level profile
attributes 67\% of GPU time to matmuls and 16\% to elementwise work, which is where a fused
adaLN kernel would have to earn its keep.

\end{abstract}

%=============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================================

Diffusion models are usually consumed through a library: a scheduler object, a pretrained
backbone, a pipeline. This report takes the opposite route. Everything is built from scratch,
including the forward and reverse processes, the two backbones, both training objectives, the
samplers and the evaluation.

The study asks three questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (architecture).} At a similar parameter scale, does replacing a UNet
        with a transformer (DiT) backbone help or hurt generation quality and computational
        efficiency?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (objective and sampler).} How does a rectified-flow objective change the
        quality--NFE trade-off, and how much does sampler choice alone buy for a fixed
        trained model (DDPM ancestral vs.\ DDIM)?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (systems).} How much training and inference throughput can be recovered
        by mixed precision, compilation and kernel-level fusion at this scale --- and where
        does a distributed strategy stop being worth it?
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:background} puts the three objectives on a common footing.
Section~\ref{sec:implementation} describes the implementation,
Section~\ref{sec:setup} the frozen protocol and the evaluation protocol,
Sections~\ref{sec:samplers}--\ref{sec:systems} the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:failures} collects the experiments that failed, which were as instructive
as the ones that worked.

\section{Background: one path, three objectives}
\label{sec:background}
%=============================================================================

All three methods place the data and the noise at the ends of an interpolation path and
train a network to regress a target along it. They differ in the path, in the target, and in
the solver used to walk back from noise to data.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{The three objectives on a common footing. $\bar\alpha_t$ is the usual DDPM
  cumulative product; the flow variant uses a continuous $t\in[0,1]$.}
  \label{tab:objectives}
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    Method & Path $x_t$ & Target & Sampler (NFE) \\
    \midrule
    DDPM~\citep{ho2020ddpm} & $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$
      & $\epsilon$ & ancestral, stochastic (1000) \\
    DDIM~\citep{song2020ddim} & same & $\epsilon$ & deterministic ODE (10--50) \\
    Rectified flow~\citep{liu2022flow,lipman2022fm} & $(1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\epsilon$
      & $v = \epsilon - x_0$ & Euler ODE (4--128) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \fig[width=2.65in]{figures/paths.pdf}
  \caption{The diffusion path and the rectified-flow path between data and noise. The flow
  objective regresses a constant velocity along a straight line; the diffusion objective
  traverses a curved, non-uniform-speed path.}
  \label{fig:paths}
\end{figure}

Both backbones embed the timestep with a sinusoidal basis calibrated for $t \in [0, 1000]$, the
DDPM range. The flow code keeps its path mathematics in $t\in[0,1]$ and passes $t\times1000$ to
the network; without that scaling the embedding is nearly constant and conditioning collapses.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
%=============================================================================

Everything is written in PyTorch, with no generative-model library. Table~\ref{tab:models}
lists the measured sizes. The parameter ranking and the FLOPs ranking disagree across scales:
on CelebA the UNet is larger on both axes, while on MNIST it has fewer parameters but more
FLOPs.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{Measured sizes (forward pass, one sample at the model's native resolution).
  Params and FLOPs are counted by \texttt{eval/cost.py} with
  \texttt{torch.utils.flop\_counter}, so both backbones are measured with the same tool.}
  \label{tab:models}
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Dataset & Backbone & Params & GFLOPs/sample & Conditioning & Attention \\
    \midrule
    MNIST $28^2$ & UNet & 11.7M & 2.43 & sinusoidal + MLP & bottleneck \\
    MNIST $28^2$ & DiT  & 16.5M & 1.05 & adaLN-Zero          & all 6 blocks (49 tokens) \\
    CelebA $64^2$ & UNet & 174.1M & 67.8 & sinusoidal + MLP & bottleneck \\
    CelebA $64^2$ & DiT  & 129.6M & 43.6 & adaLN-Zero          & all 12 blocks (256 tokens) \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Backbones.} The UNet is a residual U-net with GroupNorm, SiLU, sinusoidal timestep
injection into every residual block, and \texttt{scaled\_dot\_product\_attention} at the
bottleneck. The DiT follows \citet{peebles2023dit}: patchify with a strided convolution,
fixed two-dimensional sincos positional embeddings, and blocks whose attention and MLP are
modulated by an adaLN-Zero MLP that is zero-initialized, so every block starts as the
identity (verified in \texttt{tests/test\_sanity.py}: the untrained model outputs exactly
zero). Attention uses PyTorch SDPA in both backbones; no fused-attention library is used.

\textbf{Training.} A single training loop drives both objectives. It supports torchrun DDP,
YAML configs, checkpoint resume, an exponential moving average of the weights (all reported
FID uses EMA weights), per-epoch EMA-only snapshots for quality-vs-progress curves, and cost
accounting: parameters, measured FLOPs, step time, throughput, peak memory and MFU are
written to \texttt{results/<run>.json} on every run.

\textbf{Samplers.} Ancestral DDPM sampling, deterministic DDIM sampling with a configurable
NFE, and Euler integration of the flow ODE. All three reuse the same trained \(\epsilon\)- or
velocity-predicting network, so they can be compared at fixed weights.

%=============================================================================
\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{Hardware.} One node with six NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs (48\,GB each) connected over
PCIe with \emph{no} NVLink. The GPUs span two NUMA domains --- 0--3 on one socket, 4--5 on
the other --- and the cross-domain link traverses PCIe \emph{and} the inter-socket
interconnect. This topology is why every multi-GPU measurement in this report runs inside a
single domain and sets \texttt{NCCL\_P2P\_DISABLE=1} (Section~\ref{sec:failures}).

The node is shared. During these runs four other training jobs held between 0.5 and 47\,GB on
the remaining cards, so the GPU was never exclusively ours. The single-GPU settings sweep (30
steps plus five discarded warmups, coefficient of variation 0.1--0.6\%) is the least exposed to
that contention; the throughput columns of the FID tables are global rates from 4-GPU sharded
sweeps and should be read as such. Software: Python 3.11 and PyTorch 2.13.0 (+cu126).

\textbf{Protocol.} All MNIST cells share one frozen protocol: identical optimizer,
learning-rate schedule, batch size (global), EMA decay and seed (Table~\ref{tab:protocol}).
Unlike recipes that tune each cell separately, this design means a difference between cells
is attributable to the backbone or the objective rather than to the optimizer.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{The frozen protocol. Any deviation is recorded in the run's JSON.}
  \label{tab:protocol}
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    & MNIST $28^2$ & CelebA $64^2$ \\
    \midrule
    Cells & \multicolumn{2}{l}{UNet/DiT $\times$ $\epsilon$/flow, one run each} \\
    Epochs & 200 & 300 \\
    Global batch & 256 & 800 (160 per GPU $\times$ 5) \\
    Optimizer & \multicolumn{2}{l}{Adam, lr $2\times10^{-4}$, warmup then cosine to $10^{-6}$} \\
    Gradient clipping & \multicolumn{2}{l}{1.0} \\
    EMA decay & \multicolumn{2}{l}{0.9999, applied in every cell} \\
    Seed & \multicolumn{2}{l}{42} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{FID protocol.} Reference statistics are computed from the training tensor itself
(\texttt{data/celeba\_64\_uint8.pt} for CelebA, the MNIST training split for MNIST) with the
same preprocessing chain used to evaluate samples; third-party statistics are never used,
because any difference in resizing makes the numbers incomparable. CelebA uses the standard
Inception-v3 pool3 features at $299^2$. Inception features are not meaningful for
grayscale digits, so the MNIST cells are evaluated in the feature space of a small
convolutional classifier trained on MNIST inside this repository
(Table~\ref{tab:protocol} footnote); that metric is \emph{non-standard} and only comparable
within this report. Tier~1 evaluations use 10k samples, Tier~2 (final) 50k, and the spread
is reported as the variability across five random 10k subsets.

\subsection{What a better sampler buys, at CelebA scale}

The practical promise of DDIM is that a fixed trained model can be sampled in far fewer
network evaluations. Table~\ref{tab:celeba-samplers} answers that for the existing CelebA
DDPM checkpoint (UNet, 174.1M parameters, 300 epochs, trained before this protocol freeze).
Quality improves monotonically with the budget, and the 50-step deterministic sampler lands
within 1\% of the 1000-step ancestral sampler while running $20\times$ faster: the last 950
evaluations of the ancestral chain buy 0.1 FID points and cost 7.1 hours per 50k-sample
evaluation. The 10-step sampler is the other end of the trade --- $5\times$ fewer evaluations
than the 50-step sampler for $2.6\times$ the FID.

A 10k subset reads about 1 FID unit \emph{higher} than the 50k measurement of the same sampler
($12.41 \pm 0.08$ vs.\ $11.45$ for DDIM at 50 NFE), so the 10k ancestral number in the last row
is if anything pessimistic: on equal footing the gap between the two rows is smaller than the
table's raw numbers suggest. This sample-count dependence is why the deterministic rows here
are reported at 50k and the expensive ancestral row is not.

\subsection{Quality--NFE frontier on MNIST}

Table~\ref{tab:mnist-nfe} is the same measurement for all four MNIST cells, which share a
training budget and protocol. The rectified-flow cells dominate $\epsilon$-prediction once the
budget exceeds $\sim$16 evaluations: at 64 evaluations the flow cells reach FID $0.88$ (UNet)
and $4.99$ (DiT), where the best $\epsilon$-prediction result is $4.32$ at 50. Every curve is
monotone in the budget except the transformer's $\epsilon$ cell, which is the subject of the
next subsection.

\subsection{The deterministic sampler collapses on the transformer}

Cell B is the one place in this study where more computation makes the result worse: its FID
\emph{grows} from 49.1 at NFE 10 to 159.7 at 20 and 672.4 at 50, while 1000-step ancestral
sampling of the same weights gives 33.2. Four checks localize the cause.

\textbf{Not the weights.} Sampling the raw (non-EMA) weights reproduces the failure
(FID 273.5 and 274.6 at NFE 20 and 50), so the exponential moving average is not at fault.

\textbf{Not the model.} The identical checkpoint with the stochastic ancestral sampler
reaches 33.2, and the training-time sample grids (ancestral, EMA weights) are recognizable
digits. The transformer learned the distribution well enough to be sampled.

\textbf{Not the sampler code.} The same function improves the UNet monotonically
($22.06 \rightarrow 4.57 \rightarrow 4.32$); its arithmetic does not depend on the backbone.

\textbf{It is the missing noise injection.} DDIM's $\eta$ interpolates between the
deterministic update ($\eta = 0$) and the ancestral posterior variance ($\eta = 1$). At fixed
NFE $=50$, raising $\eta$ restores the data statistics monotonically
(Table~\ref{tab:eta}) and turns the samples from saturated blobs back into digits.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{DiT $+$ $\epsilon$, DDIM at NFE $=50$, as a function of the noise-injection level
  $\eta$. ``Saturated'' is the fraction of pixels pinned at $\pm1$ after the final clamp;
  MNIST digits are near-binary, so the ancestral sampler (NFE 1000, $\eta=1$) saturates 84.8\%
  of pixels. Nothing else about the sampler changes.}
  \label{tab:eta}
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    $\eta$ & 0 & 0.25 & 0.50 & 0.75 & 1.00 \\

    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why this happens.} The deterministic recursion integrates the model's
$\epsilon$ error coherently along the trajectory, whereas each ancestral step re-injects the
schedule's noise and thereby bounds the drift. The transformer's error is large enough for
that difference to matter: measured against the same noised batches
(\texttt{eval/eps\_error\_by\_t.py}), its $\epsilon$ MSE is 30--55\% higher than the UNet's
across the whole trajectory, with the gap widest at low noise levels --- precisely where the
deterministic update reads its final image directly out of the model's $x_0$ estimate. The
same sampler that is the cheapest way to sample the UNet is the worst way to sample the
transformer, and the transformer is the model whose per-sample cost is lowest.

DDIM is not fragile in general: it is the best sampler in Table~\ref{tab:celeba-samplers} and
on the UNet. Its NFE reduction is conditional on score accuracy, and the condition is invisible
unless both backbones are measured.

\section{Backbone and objective (RQ1)}
\label{sec:backbone}
%=============================================================================

Table~\ref{tab:2x2} is the headline comparison: four cells, one protocol, one dataset, and a
FID for every one of them. The UNet wins both rows and both columns. Against the
$\epsilon$-objective it wins by $11.9\times$ ($2.79$ vs.\ $33.18$); against rectified flow it
wins by $5.7\times$ ($0.88$ vs.\ $4.99$). The ordering holds at every sampling budget we
measured (Table~\ref{tab:mnist-nfe}), so it is not an artefact of one sampler.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{The 2$\times$2 design on MNIST, at the best budget measured for each cell
  (1000-step ancestral for the $\epsilon$ cells, 64-step Euler for the flow cells; both
  10k samples, EMA weights). Sizes are measured, not configured, and by the same tool for
  both backbones.}
  \label{tab:2x2}
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    Cell & Backbone $+$ objective & Params & GFLOPs/sample & Best FID & NFE & s/step \\
    \midrule
    A & UNet $+$ $\epsilon$ & 11.7M & 2.43 & \textbf{2.79} & 1000 & 0.101 \\
    B & DiT $+$ $\epsilon$  & 16.5M & 1.08 & 33.18 & 1000 & 0.073 \\
    C & UNet $+$ flow       & 11.7M & 2.43 & \textbf{0.88} & 64 & 0.102 \\
    D & DiT $+$ flow        & 16.5M & 1.08 &  4.99 & 64 & 0.072 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Parameters are not compute.} The DiT has \emph{more} parameters (16.5M vs.\ 11.7M)
and \emph{less} compute (1.08 vs.\ 2.43 GFLOPs per sample): its parameters sit in a wide MLP
over 49 tokens, while the UNet spends its compute on convolutions at $28^2$. The comparison
is therefore not compute-matched, and the mismatch favours the winner --- it would be a
stronger claim if the two cells had equal FLOPs. This is the compute-matched experiment the
report does not run.

\textbf{The objective comparison is the cleaner of the two.} Cells C and D differ from A and
B only in the regression target and the sampler, and the same ordering appears at every
budget: rectified flow reaches FID $0.88$ where $\epsilon$-prediction plateaus at $4.32$. The
flow objective buys an $8\times$ reduction in evaluations at the cheap end of the curve and a
$4.9\times$ lower FID at the expensive end. A straighter path is easier to integrate, and the
effect reproduces on both backbones.

\textbf{Mixed precision is the whole story.} BF16 buys $3.15\times$ the step rate and $1.6\times$
less peak memory, and nothing else we tried moves the needle. That single number is what makes
the larger-scale experiments in this project affordable at all: the CelebA cells below cost
$\sim$36 and $\sim$55 GPU-hours in fp32 and under 12 and 18 in bf16.

\textbf{The speedup is not free.} One MNIST cell was retrained under the identical protocol
with the training step autocast to bf16, to price the speedup rather than assume it. The
training loss is unchanged to three digits ($0.0194$ vs.\ $0.0193$), but the sampled quality is
not: the bf16 checkpoint scores FID $5.56$ where the fp32 one scores $2.79$ on the 1000-step
ancestral sampler, while the two sit within noise of each other at 50 steps ($4.10$ vs.\
$4.32$). This is the signature Section~\ref{sec:samplers} found for the transformer, an error
the long trajectory accumulates and the short one hides, appearing here from precision rather
than from training. One cell and one seed is thin evidence, but it is enough that we do not
quote $3.15\times$ as free, and it is why no quality number in this report comes from a
mixed-precision run.

\textbf{Compilation buys nothing here.} \texttt{torch.compile} returns $1.00\times$, the same
step time to three digits. Two explanations are consistent with the profile: 67\% of GPU time
is already inside cuBLAS matmuls that have little to fuse, and the fusible elementwise work
sits between large kernels rather than accumulating in a long chain.

\textbf{Why FSDP loses at this scale.} FSDP is $12.8\%$ slower than DDP and saves 4.8\% of
peak memory. The peak is dominated by activations: at batch 160 the model's parameters,
gradients and Adam state together account for roughly 2\,GB of the 33.5\,GB peak, so sharding
them cannot help, while the all-gather and reduce-scatter on the critical path cost real time.
Sharding pays when the model's \emph{states} rather than its activations set the ceiling, which
at 130M parameters on 48\,GB cards they do not.

\textbf{Reading the MFU column.} The $10.4\%$ fp32 and $32.7\%$ bf16 figures come from measured
FLOPs (46.0 GFLOPs per sample, forward, counted by \texttt{torch.utils.flop\_counter}) against
the A6000's dense peak. The counter covers matmul and convolution work only, so elementwise
operations are invisible to it; a fused-adaLN kernel would have to earn its keep out of the
15.3\% slice in Fig.~\ref{fig:profile}, not out of the FLOPs number. The model also runs at 256
tokens with batch 160, which is not enough of either to saturate tensor cores, so bf16 moves
this workload from barely using the tensor cores to using them moderately.

%=============================================================================
\section{What didn't work}
\label{sec:failures}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{Learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$ collapses training.} The first CelebA run used
$5\times10^{-4}$ on the assumption that Adam's normalisation made it safe. The loss diverged
and the samples degraded into noise, leaving no usable checkpoint: at that rate the early
updates in the high-noise regime leave the region where the $\epsilon$-prediction loss is
informative, and the optimiser does not return. This protocol uses $2\times10^{-4}$ with
gradient clipping at 1.0 and logs the gradient norm every step, so the failure is visible
before the loss is. Every cell in this report shares that pair of settings.

\textbf{DataParallel deadlocks with a multi-worker DataLoader.} The first multi-GPU
configuration used \texttt{nn.DataParallel} and hung reliably, with no error output, whenever
the DataLoader used worker processes. DataParallel replicates the module per forward pass and
serialises it through one process, which interacts badly with forked workers pinning memory.
The stack was rewritten around torchrun-native DDP, which every run here uses.

\textbf{P2P hangs on this node's NUMA layout.} A hang remained after the move to DDP, and
\texttt{nvidia-smi topo -m} explained it: the six GPUs split across two NUMA domains (0--3 and
4--5), with the cross-domain link traversing PCIe and the inter-socket interconnect. A job
spanning both domains hung in its first collective; a job confined to one domain ran. NCCL's
peer-to-peer transport does not complete over that link, so every multi-GPU measurement here
sets \texttt{NCCL\_P2P\_DISABLE=1} and stays inside one domain. On shared hardware the topology
is part of the experiment setup, and measuring it once is cheaper than debugging it later.

\textbf{FSDP is a net loss at this scale.} Measured in Table~\ref{tab:cost} and accounted for
in Section~\ref{sec:systems}. It stays in the report because the criterion is the useful part:
sharding pays when a model's optimiser states, not its activations, set the memory ceiling.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%=============================================================================

The three questions have different levels of support behind them. RQ2 is answered with a
complete sweep at both scales. RQ3 is answered with measurements on one model and one
hardware configuration, which is enough to establish the ranking of the levers (mixed
precision $\gg$ everything else; compilation nothing; sharding negative) but not to
generalise the constants. RQ1 has the weakest footing: the 2$\times$2 is complete at MNIST
scale, the comparison is not compute-matched, and there is no second scale --- so the
resulting ordering should be read as "at this size, under this protocol, with this much
compute" rather than as a claim about transformers in general.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Unconditional generation only: no classifier-free guidance, no text conditioning,
        no latent space.
  \item MNIST carries the full 2$\times$2; CelebA contributes a sampler study on an existing
        checkpoint. The CelebA cells were estimated at $\sim$36\,h (DiT) and $\sim$55\,h
        (UNet) each in fp32 and were not run.
  \item The A/B and C/D comparisons are not compute-matched: the UNet has $2.25\times$ the
        FLOPs per sample, so its win is achieved with more compute, not less.
  \item FID is computed against reference statistics built from this repository's own
        preprocessing chain. Absolute values are \emph{not} comparable with published
        numbers that use different resizing; only comparisons within this report are
        meaningful.
  \item The MNIST metric is a non-standard feature-space distance (a small CNN trained
        on MNIST for \texttt{eval/fid.py}), chosen because Inception features carry little
        information about grayscale digits.
  \item One seed per cell. The reported spread is FID variability across five random
        subsets, which is what the 10k-sample protocol would have given --- not a
        confidence interval on the model.
  \item FLOPs counts cover matmul and convolution work only, so the MFU figures are a
        matmul-utilisation proxy rather than a hardware limit.
  \item The reported timings use fp32 training, and no quality number in this report comes
        from a mixed-precision run. bf16 was measured for speed (Table~\ref{tab:cost}) and,
        in one cell, for quality: the training loss matched to three digits while the
        1000-step FID doubled. That is one seed and one cell, so it locates a failure mode
        without marking where it starts.
  \item The transformer's deterministic-sampler collapse (Section~\ref{sec:samplers}) is
        characterised at one scale with one model. The $\eta$ sweep localises the mechanism to
        the absence of noise injection; whether the boundary moves with more training or more
        parameters is not measured.
\end{itemize}
